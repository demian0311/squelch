# FM radio

Broadcast stations on 88–108 MHz: music, news and talk, each on its own 200 kHz channel. The strongest signals this receiver will ever see.

## What you will hear

- A station every 200 kHz on the odd tenths (88.1, 88.3 … 107.9). 88–92 MHz is reserved for noncommercial stations: college, public radio, religious.
- Stations run up to 100 kW, so a city has thirty or more in reach and a car hears them 50 miles out.
- The Stations panel lists every licensed station near your location from the [FCC's own list](https://www.fcc.gov/media/radio/fm-query), with the ones in the waterfall's view marked.

## How this radio reads it

Wide FM. The signal carries more than the sound: a 19 kHz pilot tone says "stereo", the difference between left and right rides above it, and RDS (Radio Data System) is a slow data stream at 57 kHz with the station's name, the song title and the programme type. The card above a station fills in as RDS comes in.

![What one FM station carries, by frequency above the carrier](diagram/fm-mpx)

Many stations also send HD Radio: a digital copy of the station, plus extra channels, in two narrow bands 200 kHz either side of the analog signal. The card's HD chip lights when both sidebands are heard; click it and the digital sound replaces the analog after a few seconds.

![HD Radio sits beside the analog station](diagram/fm-hd)

## Band plan

| MHz | What |
|---|---|
| 88.1–91.9 | Noncommercial: public radio, college, religious |
| 92.1–107.9 | Commercial stations |
| 200 kHz | Channel spacing; stations sit on the odd tenths |
| ±200 kHz | HD Radio digital sidebands |

## Rules

- [FCC Part 73](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/part-73) (broadcast). Listening needs nothing.
- Only a licensed station may transmit here. Low-power FM (LPFM, 100 W) licences go to community groups, not individuals.
- [Part 15](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/part-15) lets a tiny transmitter (a car phone adapter) reach a few feet.
