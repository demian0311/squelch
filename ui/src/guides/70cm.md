# 70 cm

420–450 MHz: the second home of local ham radio. Repeaters, satellites, amateur television and most of the digital voice modes.

## What you will hear

- FM repeaters between 442 and 450 MHz, many linked to a 2 m repeater or to the internet.
- 446.0 MHz simplex, the national calling frequency.
- Digital voice: DMR, Fusion and D-STAR repeaters live here more than anywhere. Analog FM hears them as a buzz; a repeater listed as DMR opens in this receiver's DMR decoder.
- Satellites between 435 and 438 MHz, and the space station's cross-band repeater at 437.800 MHz.

## How this radio reads it

Narrow FM. Repeaters are 5 MHz from their inputs: outputs 442–445 MHz listen 5 MHz above, outputs 447–450 listen 5 MHz below. The band is 30 MHz, so the waterfall shows a slice; the Repeaters panel finds the ones near you.

## Band plan

| MHz | What |
|---|---|
| 420–432 | Amateur television |
| 432.100 | SSB and Morse calling |
| 435–438 | Satellites only |
| 437.800 | Space station cross-band repeater output |
| 442–445 | FM repeater outputs, inputs 5 MHz above |
| 446.000 | National FM simplex calling |
| 447–450 | FM repeater outputs, inputs 5 MHz below |

## Rules

- [FCC Part 97](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/part-97). Listening needs nothing.
- Transmitting needs an amateur licence. [Technician](https://www.arrl.org/getting-licensed), the first of three classes, is a 35-question exam, no Morse, $35 to the FCC for ten years, and it covers this whole band.
- A station sends its callsign at least every ten minutes and at the end of a contact (97.119), so you always learn who is talking.
- Up to 1.5 kW is legal, but a repeater runs tens of watts and a handheld five.
- Power is limited near some military radar sites (the FCC lists them in [97.303](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/97.303)).
