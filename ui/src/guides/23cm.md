# 23 cm

1240–1300 MHz, the top of this dongle's reach and the bottom of microwave ham radio. Repeaters, amateur television, satellite uplinks, and the moon.

## What you will hear

- FM repeaters near 1282–1288 MHz, rare outside big cities.
- 1294.5 MHz, the FM simplex calling frequency.
- SSB and Morse at 1296.1 MHz; the EME (moonbounce) crowd just below it on weekends.
- Amateur television: a wide signal you see on the waterfall and cannot hear.
- Satellites use 1260–1270 MHz for uplinks only: nothing comes down here.

## How this radio reads it

Narrow FM. Repeaters talk 12 MHz above their inputs. Signals are weak and antennas small; a repeater is a line of sight thing. The band is 60 MHz, so the waterfall shows a slice.

## Band plan

| MHz | What |
|---|---|
| 1240–1282, in three slices | Amateur television |
| 1260–1270 | Satellite uplinks only |
| 1270–1276 | FM repeater inputs |
| 1282–1288 | FM repeater outputs, inputs 12 MHz below |
| 1294.500 | National FM simplex calling |
| 1296.100 | SSB and Morse calling |

## Rules

- [FCC Part 97](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/part-97). Listening needs nothing.
- Transmitting needs an amateur licence. [Technician](https://www.arrl.org/getting-licensed), the first of three classes, is a 35-question exam, no Morse, $35 to the FCC for ten years, and it covers this whole band.
- A station sends its callsign at least every ten minutes and at the end of a contact (97.119), so you always learn who is talking.
- Up to 1.5 kW is legal, but a repeater runs tens of watts and a handheld five.
- Amateur use is secondary: radar and satellite navigation come first, and power is limited near some radar sites.
