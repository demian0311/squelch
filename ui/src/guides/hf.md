# 80, 40 and 20 m

The three amateur bands this receiver reaches below its tuner: 3.5–4, 7–7.3 and 14–14.35 MHz. Voice across the country by night on 80 and 40 m, across the world by day on 20 m.

## What you will hear

- Single sideband voice: contests, nets that meet at the same hour each day, ragchews between old friends, and "CQ DX" calls to other continents.
- Morse and digital modes at the bottom of each band; FT8 is the loudest of them, a warble every 15 seconds on 3.573, 7.074 and 14.074 MHz.
- 20 m is a daytime band open to the world when the sun is up at either end. 40 m carries the country by evening and the world by night. 80 m is a night band, local by day.
- Slow-scan television (pictures sent as sound) and the few AM enthusiasts around their calling frequencies.

## How this radio reads it

Below 24 MHz the dongle samples the antenna straight (direct sampling): no tuner gain, so the antenna sets the level, and a whip hears little. Voice is lower sideband on 80 and 40 m and upper sideband on 20 m, the convention since the 1950s; the bands enter in those modes. Tune in 100 Hz steps until the voice sounds right. Morse and digital show as thin lines; the receiver does not decode them.

## Rules

- [FCC Part 97](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/part-97). Listening needs nothing.
- Who may transmit where is [97.301](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/97.301): a General may use voice on 14.225–14.350 and 7.175–7.300 MHz and the top of 80 m; an Extra has the whole of each band; a Technician has Morse only here, on 3.525–3.600 and 7.025–7.125 MHz.
- A station sends its callsign at least every ten minutes and at the end of a contact (97.119), so you always learn who is talking.
- Up to 1.5 kW is legal; most stations run 100 W.
