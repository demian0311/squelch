# 2 m

144–148 MHz, the busiest amateur band: local repeaters, club nets, position beacons, and the space station.

## What you will hear

- FM voice through repeaters on hilltops: a callsign every ten minutes by rule, a weekly net where members check in, a commute conversation.
- 146.52 MHz is the national simplex calling frequency: people call there and move off.
- 144.39 MHz is APRS, a data channel of position beacons: a chirp every few seconds, not voice.
- SSB and Morse near 144.2 MHz when someone is chasing distance.

## How this radio reads it

Narrow FM. A repeater listens 600 kHz off where it talks, so you hear the repeater, not the handhelds. The Repeaters panel marks [RepeaterBook](https://www.repeaterbook.com/)'s repeaters near your location and colours the ones in reach; a repeater listed as DMR opens in the digital decoder. The space station's voice and SSTV are at 145.800 MHz: [Space station](band:iss)

## Band plan

| MHz | What |
|---|---|
| 144.000–144.100 | Morse only (by law) |
| 144.200 | SSB calling |
| 144.390 | APRS data |
| 145.200–145.500 | FM repeater outputs, inputs 600 kHz below |
| 145.800–146.000 | Satellites and the space station |
| 146.520 | National FM simplex calling |
| 146.610–147.390 | FM repeater outputs, inputs ±600 kHz |

## Rules

- [FCC Part 97](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/part-97). Listening needs nothing.
- Transmitting needs an amateur licence. [Technician](https://www.arrl.org/getting-licensed), the first of three classes, is a 35-question exam, no Morse, $35 to the FCC for ten years, and it covers this whole band.
- A station sends its callsign at least every ten minutes and at the end of a contact (97.119), so you always learn who is talking.
- Up to 1.5 kW is legal, but a repeater runs tens of watts and a handheld five.
