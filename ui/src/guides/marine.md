# Marine

Boats, harbours and the Coast Guard on VHF between 156 and 162 MHz. Channel 16 is where every boat listens.

## What you will hear

- Channel 16: calling and distress. A boat calls another here, then they move to a working channel. The Coast Guard answers here. "Pan-pan" is urgency, "Mayday" is distress.
- Channel 13: ships talking to each other about passing, at low power; ships over 20 m keep a watch on it.
- Channels 68, 69, 71, 72 and 78A: recreational boats talking among themselves. 9: recreational calling.
- Channel 22A: Coast Guard safety broadcasts, announced on 16 first. 12 and 14: port operations and vessel traffic.
- Near the coast or a lake, weekends are busy; inland, silence.

## How this radio reads it

Narrow FM. Most channels are simplex, one frequency, so both sides come through. Channels 24–28 are duplex: the boat transmits on 157 MHz and the coast station answers on 161 MHz, two frequencies 4.6 MHz apart; the Channels panel shows the pair as two halves and the dongle hears one side at a time. Channel 70 is DSC (Digital Selective Calling): data, not voice.

## Rules

- [FCC Part 80](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/part-80). Listening needs nothing.
- A recreational boat in US waters needs no station licence for its VHF radio. Boats on international voyages, and commercial vessels, do.
- Marine radios are for boats: using a marine handheld on land is not allowed.
- 156.800 MHz is the international distress frequency; a false distress call is a federal crime.
